Why a third of feedback makes performance worse, and how to give the other kind

Most managers believe feedback improves performance. The research says it usually does, and that in more than one case in three it does the opposite. The difference comes down to where the feedback sends the person's attention.

By Hisham Elsaied, ICF PCC executive coach in Dubai  ·  November 2026

In coaching sessions I hear about feedback from both sides. Leaders tell me they gave clear, honest feedback and nothing changed. The people who received it tell me something different: they heard a judgement about who they are, and spent the next week defending themselves instead of fixing anything. Both accounts can be true at once, and the research explains why.

The study most managers have never heard of

In 1996 Avraham Kluger and Angelo DeNisi published what is still the most thorough review of feedback research. They pooled 607 effects from studies covering more than 23,000 observations, and kept only well-controlled studies that measured actual performance.

On average, feedback improved performance, with a moderate effect (d = 0.41). But in over a third of cases, performance got worse after the feedback. Not unchanged. Worse.

That finding had been sitting in the data for decades, largely ignored, because most people assume feedback is good by definition.

Where attention goes, performance follows

Kluger and DeNisi's explanation is simple and useful. Feedback moves a person's attention. It can move it towards the task (what to do differently next time), or up towards the self (am I good enough, does my manager rate me, how do I compare with my colleagues).

When feedback keeps attention on the task, it tends to help. When it pulls attention onto the person, their worth or a comparison with others, it tends to hurt, because the person's energy goes into protecting themselves. Praise can do this too. "You're a natural" points at the person, not at what they did.

So the question for a manager isn't "was I honest?" It's "where did my words send their attention?"

What this looks like in practice

Compare two versions of the same message.

Version one: "You were unprofessional in the finance meeting. You need to be more careful."

Version two: "In Tuesday's finance meeting, you presented the numbers before we'd checked them together. The CFO questioned two figures and we lost some credibility. Next time, could you send me the numbers a day before any senior meeting?"

The first is about the person and asks for a quality ("more careful") that can't be seen. The second names a moment, describes behaviour, states the impact and asks for one action anyone could check. That second shape is the Situation, Behaviour, Impact structure developed by the Center for Creative Leadership, with a clear request added at the end.

Five checks before you give feedback

  • A specific moment. If you can't name when and where, you probably have an impression rather than an example. Find an example.
  • Behaviour, not labels. Words like lazy, arrogant, careless and unprofessional are verdicts. So are "always" and "never". Describe what you saw or heard.
  • The impact on the work. Say what it meant for the team, customers or results. This is what makes the feedback worth hearing.
  • One request someone could act on. "Be more strategic" can't be done on Monday morning. "Bring two options, not one, to the next budget review" can.
  • Your own state. If you're still angry, wait. Feedback given in anger almost always lands on the person.

The Gulf adds one more check

In most workplaces I know in the region, how something is said matters as much as what is said. Corrective feedback in front of others is rarely heard as information. It's heard as public loss of face, and the relationship pays for it long after the issue is forgotten. Keep it private, keep it about the work, and leave the person a dignified way to respond. Ask how they saw it before you ask for change. You'll often learn something you didn't know.

None of this means softening the message until it disappears. Direct feedback, delivered privately and about the task, is respected here. What damages trust is feedback that feels like a judgement of the person.
