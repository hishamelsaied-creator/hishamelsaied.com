HR director readiness check

Eighteen statements, about four minutes. For HR managers, business partners and heads of HR thinking about a director role. You'll see where you're ready and what is still missing.

AWhat is your current role?
BWhere do you work?

For each statement, choose how true it is for you today. Some are worded negatively on purpose, so read each one.

1I can explain how my organisation makes money and what drives its main costs.
2Senior leaders outside HR ask for my view on business decisions.
3My team could run well for two weeks without me.
4I have led a difficult people situation, such as a restructure or a senior investigation, from start to finish.
5I use people data to make a case, not only to report activity.
6There is a senior person who would put my name forward for a director role.
7I describe HR work in terms of business results, such as revenue, cost or risk.
8I have disagreed with a senior leader, with reasons, and kept the relationship.
9Most of my time goes on setting direction, not doing the work myself.
10I stay calm and fair when a situation becomes tense or political.
11I can show what changed in the business because of an HR initiative I led.
12People outside my organisation know my work, through talks, articles or professional networks.
13Finance and budget discussions are mostly outside my comfort zone.
14I'm usually brought in after decisions are made, to implement them.
15I find it hard to hand over work I'm good at.
16I tend to avoid conversations that could upset a senior person.
17Most of the HR numbers I share are about activity, such as hires or training hours.
18Senior leaders would struggle to name what I have achieved in the last year.
The thinking behind this tool

The six areas come from research on what makes HR leaders effective, and from what I saw in twenty years of HR, including as a regional HR director, about who gets appointed.

  • Credibility and a business view. The HR Competency Study run by the RBL Group and the University of Michigan, led by Dave Ulrich, has surveyed HR professionals and the line managers they work with in several rounds since the late 1980s. Being a "credible activist", trusted by line leaders and willing to take a position on the business, came out as the most valued quality in its 2007 round. This gives the first two areas.
  • Leading through others. Each step up asks leaders to change how they spend their time and what they value, and holding on to work you are good at is a common reason people stall (Charan, Drotter and Noel, The Leadership Pipeline).
  • Judgement, evidence and visibility. These come from practice: the situations directors are trusted with, the case HR has to make in business terms, and the role of a senior sponsor in appointments.

How it's scored. Each area has three statements, one worded negatively and reverse-scored to reduce the pull to agree with everything. Area scores run from 0 to 100. The tool also checks whether your answers within an area point in different directions, and reads your result against your current role.

What it isn't. This is a structured reflection, not a validated psychometric test, and it hasn't been normed on a population. Use it to decide where to focus, then check your view with your manager, a sponsor or a coach.
