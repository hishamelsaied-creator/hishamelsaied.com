The future of work in the GCC: what changes for HR leaders by 2030

The headlines are about jobs disappearing. The harder work for HR is about skills, trust and who gets to grow.

By Hisham Elsaied, executive coach and author, Dubai  ·  November 2026

Most HR conferences this year have a session on the future of work. Many of them end with a slide of big numbers and very little about what an HR director should do differently next week. So here is an attempt at both.

What the research says

The World Economic Forum's Future of Jobs Report 2025, based on over 1,000 employers, expects around 170 million new jobs and 92 million displaced worldwide by 2030, a net gain of 78 million. Employers expect 39 percent of the skills people use at work to change in that period. Skill gaps are the barrier they name most often.

In this region, people are moving faster than the global average. PwC's Middle East Workforce Hopes and Fears Survey 2025 found that 75 percent of employees had used AI tools at work in the past year, against 69 percent globally, and 61 percent of office workers said they felt excited about AI's impact, compared with 47 percent worldwide.

And in the UAE, nationalisation is now part of everyday workforce planning. Private-sector companies with 50 or more staff have been required to raise Emiratis in skilled roles by two percent a year, reaching 10 percent by the end of 2026. The Ministry says 95 percent of covered companies met their first-half 2026 target.

What this means for HR leaders

  • Skills, not jobs, become the unit of planning. If more than a third of skills change, a job description written in 2024 will be out of date by 2028. HR needs a clearer picture of what people can do, not just what their title says.
  • Managers become the real development channel. No learning budget can keep up with that pace of change. Most growth will happen on the job, which means line managers who know how to give feedback and stretch people.
  • Trust becomes a workforce issue. People are excited about AI and worried about their jobs at the same time. If they don't trust how decisions about tools, roles and data are made, they'll hide what they know.
  • Nationalisation moves from hiring to careers. Meeting a number is the start. Keeping and growing national talent in the private sector is the harder test, and the one that matters most.

What stays the same

The core of the HR role doesn't change. Someone still has to tell the CEO what the organisation can really deliver, notice when good people are about to leave, and make difficult decisions fairly. If anything, the more of the routine work that moves to machines, the more those human parts of the job show.

Three questions for your next leadership meeting

  1. Which roles in our organisation will look most different by 2028, and are the people in them part of the conversation?
  2. How do we know what skills we actually have today?
  3. What would make our people trust us with the changes we're about to make?
