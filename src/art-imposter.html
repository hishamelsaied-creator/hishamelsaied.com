Imposter syndrome after a promotion: why it happens and what helps

The feeling that you don't really deserve the new role is common, and it tends to arrive just when you need confidence most.

By Hisham Elsaied, executive coach and author, Dubai  ·  November 2026

In short: imposter feelings are the persistent belief that your success is down to luck and that people will soon find out you're not as capable as they think. They are common among high achievers, men and women alike, and often peak in the first months of a bigger role. What helps is separating the feeling from the evidence, learning the parts of the job you don't yet know, and not working longer hours to compensate.

A newly appointed director told me in our first session that she was waiting for the CEO to realise he had made a mistake. She had been chosen over two external candidates, her first quarter had gone well, and her team liked her. None of that seemed to count. Every small error felt like proof. This is a composite of several clients, and versions of it are among the most common things I hear in the first months of a new role.

What it is, and what it isn't

Psychologists Pauline Clance and Suzanne Imes first described the "impostor phenomenon" in 1978, in a study of high-achieving women. Later research found it in men too. A systematic review in the Journal of General Internal Medicine in 2020 looked at 62 studies and found estimates of how common it is ranging from 9 to 82 percent, depending largely on which questionnaire and cut-off was used. It was common among both men and women, and across age groups from students to late-career professionals.

The same review found that imposter feelings often come with anxiety and depression, and are linked with lower job performance and satisfaction and with burnout. It is not a recognised psychiatric diagnosis, and the review found no published studies that tested treatments. So be careful with anyone who promises to "cure" it. If the feelings are affecting your sleep, your mood or your health, speak to a doctor or a qualified therapist. Coaching helps with the work side, but it is not a replacement for that.

Why it often peaks after a step up

  • You are doing a job you haven't done before. That is the point of a promotion, but it means there will be gaps, and gaps feel like proof.
  • The measures of success change. In your old role you knew what a good day looked like. In the new one, the feedback is slower and less clear.
  • Your peers changed. You now sit with people who have been at this level for years. You compare your inside with their outside.
  • People knew the old you. If you were promoted internally, you may feel that colleagues still see you in your former role.
  • You moved country or culture. Many leaders in the Gulf have changed country as well as role. Not knowing the unwritten rules adds to the feeling of being found out.

What helps

  1. Name it. Saying "this is the imposter feeling, not a fact" sounds simple, but it creates some distance between you and the thought.
  2. Ask why you were chosen. Ask your manager directly what made them pick you. Write the answer down. You will want to read it again on a hard day.
  3. Keep an evidence file. Once a week, note one thing that went well and what you did to make it happen. On a hard day you'll want something to look back on.
  4. Learn the gaps, deliberately. Pick the one part of the job you understand least and get specific help with it. Confidence grows from small successes on the hard parts, not from avoiding them.
  5. Don't compensate with hours. Working until midnight to prove you deserve the role is a common response, and it leads to exhaustion and poorer decisions. Your manager chose you for your judgement.
  6. Find peers at your level. Other new directors or GMs, inside or outside your organisation. You'll find that many of them feel the same.

When the feeling is useful

Some doubt is healthy. It means you are stretching, and it keeps you asking questions. The leaders I worry about more are the ones who never doubt themselves at all. The aim is to stop the feeling from making your decisions for you.

Questions to ask yourself

  1. What evidence would convince a fair outsider that I'm doing this job well?
  2. What am I avoiding because I'm afraid of being found out?
  3. Who could I talk to honestly about how this role really feels?
