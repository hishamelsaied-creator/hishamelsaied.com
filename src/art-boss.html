How to manage a difficult boss without losing yourself

A practical guide for leaders in the Gulf: understand what's driving your manager, have the conversation, and know when it's time to go.

By Hisham Elsaied, executive coach and former HR director, Dubai  ·  November 2026

In short: managing your boss means working consciously with them to get the best results for you, them and the organisation. Start by understanding their goals, pressures and preferred way of working, then agree expectations directly, in private. Most difficult bosses are under pressure, not out to get you. If the behaviour is abusive or crosses legal lines, it is no longer a relationship problem, and you should take it to HR or seek advice.

In my years in HR, a difficult manager was one of the reasons good people came to see me most often. As a coach, "my boss" is still one of the topics I hear most. People usually arrive with one of two plans: put up with it, or resign. There is almost always a third option, and it is worth trying first.

What "managing your boss" really means

In 1980 John Gabarro and John Kotter of Harvard Business School published an article in Harvard Business Review called "Managing Your Boss". It is still one of the most useful things written on the subject. Their point was that the relationship is one of mutual dependence: your boss depends on you too. Managing it isn't flattery or politics. It is working consciously with your manager so that you both get better results.

Their advice comes down to two kinds of understanding. First, your boss: their goals, the pressures on them, their strengths and blind spots, and how they like to work. Second, yourself: your own needs and style, and how you react to authority. Then you build a way of working that fits both, with clear expectations, regular updates, honesty and careful use of their time.

Start with what's driving them

Before you decide your boss is difficult, ask what pressure they are under. The micromanager is often someone who was burned by a surprise and now checks everything. The boss who changes direction every week may be getting mixed signals from above. The absent boss may be stretched across too many things.

This doesn't excuse bad behaviour. It does change how you respond, and it usually shows you where you have more influence than you thought.

Common types, and what tends to work

  • The micromanager. Give updates before they ask. Agree a rhythm, for example a short written update every Thursday, and keep to it. Trust usually follows predictability.
  • The absent boss. Ask for fifteen minutes a fortnight and come with two or three decisions you need. Confirm what was agreed in a short email afterwards.
  • The boss who changes direction. When a new instruction arrives, ask what it replaces. "Should I pause the project you asked for last week?" makes the trade-off visible without arguing.
  • The boss who takes credit. Make your work visible in ways that don't depend on them: send progress notes to the wider team, and present your own work when you can.
  • The boss with a temper. Don't respond in the moment. Come back later, in private, and talk about the effect on the work rather than on your feelings.

In the Gulf, how you raise it matters

In many Gulf organisations hierarchy is respected, and public disagreement with a senior person can cost them face and you a great deal. That doesn't mean staying silent. It means choosing the setting with care.

  • Raise concerns in private, never in front of others.
  • Talk in person, not by email. A difficult message in writing can be read as a challenge and forwarded.
  • Show respect first. Start with what you value in their leadership and what you both want for the team. It's not flattery if you mean it.
  • Be careful about going around them. Taking a complaint to their manager before you have talked to them directly is rarely forgotten.

This is for everyday friction. If you're being harassed, threatened or discriminated against, you don't need to raise it with your manager first. Go to HR or use the grievance process directly.

How to have the conversation

Ask for a private meeting with a neutral reason: "I'd like twenty minutes to talk about how we work together, so I can support you better." Then use a simple structure.

  1. Shared goal. "I want this project to succeed, and I know you do too."
  2. One specific example. "In Tuesday's meeting, the plan changed after I had briefed the team."
  3. The effect on the work. "The team lost two days and some confidence in the plan."
  4. A request. "Could we agree that changes come to me first, so I can manage them with the team?"
  5. Their view. "Is there something I could be doing differently that would help you?"

The last question matters. It often brings out what's really going on, and it makes the conversation two-way.

When it's time to go

Some situations are not yours to fix. If your manager humiliates you, threatens you, discriminates against you or asks you to do something unethical or unlawful, this is no longer about managing the relationship. Keep a factual record, use your organisation's grievance or whistleblowing process, and take professional advice if needed.

And if you've honestly tried for several months and nothing has changed, leaving can be the right decision. If you go, go by choice and with your reputation intact.

Questions to ask yourself

  1. What is my manager most worried about right now?
  2. What part of this pattern am I contributing to, even without meaning to?
  3. What one specific change would make the biggest difference, and have I asked for it clearly?
