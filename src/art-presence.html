Executive presence in the Gulf: what it is and how to build it

Why it decides promotions, what the research measures, and what changes in a Gulf boardroom.

By Hisham Elsaied, executive coach and author, Dubai  ·  November 2026

In short: executive presence is whether senior people trust you with high-stakes situations. Research puts most of its weight on how you act under pressure, then on how you communicate, with appearance a distant third. In the Gulf, calm, respect for seniority and well-chosen words usually count for more than speaking often or loudly. It can be learned, and it is built in specific moments, not in general.

"She's very capable, but she isn't ready for the leadership team." I heard versions of this sentence many times in talent reviews when I was an HR director. When I asked what "not ready" meant, the answer was usually some form of "presence". It is one of the most common reasons capable people are held back, and one of the least explained.

What the research measures

The best-known research comes from economist Sylvia Ann Hewlett and the Center for Talent Innovation, now Coqual. In 2012 they surveyed nearly 4,000 college-educated professionals, including 268 senior executives, and ran focus groups and interviews. The executives said executive presence accounts for 26 percent of what it takes to get the next promotion.

Hewlett grouped it into three parts: how you act (gravitas), how you speak (communication) and how you look (appearance). The weighting was uneven. Gravitas was named as the core by 67 percent of the senior executives, communication by 28 percent and appearance by just 5 percent. Appearance still mattered, but mainly as a first filter before people judged the rest.

A follow-up survey in 2022 found that the parts had stayed the same while what people looked for inside them had shifted, with authenticity and inclusiveness counting for more. One caution: both surveys were of US executives. The three parts travel well. How they show up in practice varies by culture.

What changes in the Gulf

I have worked in boardrooms in the Gulf, Europe and North America. These are the differences I notice most, in many Gulf organisations, not all.

  • Speaking less often can carry more weight. In many meetings the most senior person speaks last. A leader who fills every silence can come across as junior, however good the content.
  • Respect for seniority is expected. How you greet and address senior people, and how you handle disagreement with them, is noticed. Disagreeing in private first is often wiser than challenging in the meeting.
  • Relationships come before the agenda. A leader who rushes to the slides can seem cold. Time spent on the person is not wasted time.
  • Language choices matter. Leaders who move well between Arabic and English, and know when to use which, often have an advantage. Arabic can carry warmth and respect that is hard to match in a second language.
  • Calm under pressure is prized. Losing your temper, or showing panic, in front of senior people is very hard to recover from.

What people get wrong

  • Thinking presence means being loud. Talking more is the most common response to being told you need more presence. It usually makes things worse.
  • Hiding behind slides. Forty slides signal that you aren't sure what matters. Senior people want your view and the one or two facts behind it.
  • Copying someone else's style. People can tell when a style is borrowed. Work on a stronger version of your own.

How to build it

  1. Know your subject several questions deep. Senior people often ask a second and third follow-up question. Before an important meeting, write down the five hardest questions you could be asked, and your answers.
  2. Lead with the answer. Open with your recommendation in one sentence, then give the reason. Most people do it the other way round.
  3. Prepare the first two minutes. First impressions in a meeting form fast. Know exactly how you will open, and say it out loud beforehand.
  4. Practise the pause. When challenged, take a breath before answering. A short silence reads as confidence. A rushed answer reads as defence.
  5. Ask for specific feedback. Ask two people who see you in senior meetings: "What is one thing I do that adds to how I'm seen, and one thing that takes away from it?" Vague feedback is useless, so ask for an example.
  6. Watch yourself. Record a short presentation on your phone and watch it with the sound off, then listen without the picture. You will learn more in ten minutes than from a book.

Questions to ask yourself

  1. In which meeting do I feel least like myself, and what happens to me there?
  2. What would the most senior person in my organisation say about how I come across?
  3. Which one habit, if I changed it this month, would make the biggest difference?
