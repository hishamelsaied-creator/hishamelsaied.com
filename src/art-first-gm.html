First-time general manager: the mistakes I see most

What made you excellent at running a function can get in the way when you run the whole business.

By Hisham Elsaied, executive coach and author, Dubai  ·  November 2026

A new general manager, promoted from sales, spends his first month in customer meetings because that is where he feels useful. Revenue holds up. Meanwhile operations and finance are waiting for decisions only he can make, and his old sales team keeps coming to him instead of their new manager. By month four he is exhausted and wondering why the job feels so much harder than it looked.

This is a composite, but I've seen it many times, in many functions.

Why the jump is so big

Michael Watkins, who wrote The First 90 Days, described this move in Harvard Business Review as a set of seven seismic shifts. Among them: from specialist to generalist, from analyst to integrator, from tactician to strategist, and from problem solver to agenda setter. Each one asks you to let go of something that used to make you successful.

The mistakes I see most

  1. Staying in your old function. It feels productive and safe. It also tells everyone else that their area matters less, and it takes the job from the person you promoted into your old seat.
  2. Solving every problem yourself. Your job now is to decide which problems the business should be working on, and to build a team that solves them.
  3. Underestimating the other functions. You don't need to be an expert in finance, operations or HR. You do need to understand how they think well enough to ask the right questions and spot when something is off.
  4. Forgetting you're now on stage. People read every comment and every mood. A passing remark in a corridor can become a rumour by the afternoon.
  5. Treating relationships upwards as an afterthought. Owners, board members and head office need to hear from you before they need to ask.

What helps

  • A clear first conversation with your boss about what success looks like in your first year, and what they expect you to stop doing.
  • One trusted person in each function who will tell you what's really going on.
  • Regular time to step back. An hour a week with no meetings, just thinking about where the business is heading. It can feel indulgent, but it's part of the job.
  • Someone outside the organisation to think with. A mentor, a peer or a coach. The new role is lonelier than most people expect.

Questions for your first month

  1. What am I still doing that someone in my team should be doing?
  2. Which function do I understand least, and who can teach me?
  3. What will the business look like in three years if I get this right?
