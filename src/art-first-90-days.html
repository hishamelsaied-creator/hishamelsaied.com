Your first 90 days as an HR director in the Gulf

The CEO is watching. So is your team. Most of what decides how they see you happens before you've changed anything.

By Hisham Elsaied, executive coach and author, Dubai  ·  November 2026

A new HR director arrives with a plan. In week two she presents it to the executive committee: a new performance system, a restructure of the HR team and a culture survey. The CEO nods. By month three, half the committee is quietly working around her. Nobody disagreed with the plan. They just didn't know her yet, and she hadn't learned how decisions are really made in that building.

She is a composite of several people I've coached.

Why the first months matter so much

Research McKinsey draws on in its work on senior leadership transitions found that two years on, between 27 and 46 percent are seen as failures or disappointments. The main reason is rarely technical. In their findings, 68 percent of transitions that founder do so on politics, culture and people. The same research notes that tailored coaching and a proper plan for the transition can double the chance of success, yet only around a third of organisations offer either.

The 90 days aren't a deadline. Most senior people take longer than that to be fully up to speed. They're the period in which people decide what kind of leader you are.

What I'd do in the first month

  • Meet every member of the executive team one to one. Ask what they need from HR that they're not getting, and what they'd stop if they could. Write it down and say it back to them later.
  • Find out how decisions are really made. In many Gulf organisations the formal committee is only part of the picture. Who does the CEO call before a decision? Whose opinion travels?
  • Spend time with your own team before you judge it. They know what has been tried before and why it didn't work. They'll also be watching whether you respect what they've built.

Months two and three

  • Pick one or two early wins the business cares about, not the ones HR cares about. A hiring bottleneck fixed, a difficult case handled well, a cleaner Emiratisation or localisation report. Small and visible beats big and slow.
  • Agree with the CEO what success looks like at twelve months. Two or three things, in their words. Revisit them every quarter.
  • Only then bring the bigger plan. By now it will be shaped by what you heard, and people will recognise their own words in it.

Three traps I see often

  1. Copying what worked in your last company. The policy may be good. The context is different, and people notice when you haven't asked.
  2. Becoming the CEO's person too fast. If the other executives see you as the CEO's messenger, they'll stop telling you the truth.
  3. Disappearing into operations. Payroll issues and visa queries will fill every hour if you let them. Protect time for the conversations that build your seat at the table.

Questions to take into your first month

  1. Who has the most to lose if HR changes, and have I spoken to them yet?
  2. What does the CEO actually mean when they say "strategic HR"?
  3. What will I be able to point to at day 90 that the business, not HR, will care about?
