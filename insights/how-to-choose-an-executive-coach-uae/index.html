How to choose an executive coach in the UAE

Anyone can call themselves a coach. Here is what I would check before you hire one, for yourself or for a leader in your organisation.

By Hisham Elsaied, ICF PCC executive coach in Dubai  ·  November 2026

"Coach" isn't a protected title. Nobody needs a licence to use it, and in the UAE it now appears on thousands of LinkedIn profiles. Some of those people are excellent. Some are motivational speakers, trainers or life coaches who have moved into executive work without much idea of how a board, a ministry or a restructure actually works.

I spent many years on the buying side, as the HR leader who chose coaches for other people, before I became a coach myself. These are the checks I wish someone had given me.

1. A recognised credential

The International Coaching Federation (ICF) is the best known. EMCC is also respected. A credential doesn't make someone a great coach, but it shows they have been trained, assessed on real coaching and bound by a code of ethics. It also gives you somewhere to go if something goes wrong. Ask which credential they hold and check it on the awarding body's directory.

2. Real coaching hours, not training hours

A weekend course isn't experience. Ask how many hours they have spent coaching actual clients, and at what level. Coaching a first-time supervisor and coaching a director who reports to a CEO are different jobs.

3. Experience of the client's world

In the Gulf I'd say this is the most overlooked check. A coach doesn't need to have done the client's exact job. But if the client's days are full of boards, budgets, government stakeholders and headcount decisions, a coach who has seen that from the inside will be taken seriously much faster.

4. Fit with the culture

Most coaching models were built in Western Europe and North America. Many of their ideas travel well. Some don't: the idea that a coach should never offer a view, that goals belong only to the individual, or that blunt feedback is always better. Ask the coach how they adapt their approach for leaders in this region. A good answer is specific. A vague answer tells you something too.

5. Language

Many leaders here run their meetings in English and think through hard decisions in Arabic. If the topics are personal, such as confidence, family expectations or a difficult relationship with a boss, a coach who can work in Arabic can make a real difference. It isn't essential for everyone, but it is worth asking.

6. A chemistry meeting

Research on executive coaching keeps finding that the relationship between coach and client matters a great deal, especially early on. So let the leader meet two or three coaches briefly before choosing. It costs little and it is one of the most reliable ways to improve results.

Warning signs

  • Promises of "transformation" before they know the client.
  • More talk about themselves than about the client.
  • No clear answer on confidentiality: what the organisation will hear and what it won't.
  • No goals agreed at the start, so sessions drift for months.
  • No way of checking at the end whether anything changed.

If you're in HR

Agree two or three specific goals in a short three-way meeting with the leader and their manager. About three months is usually long enough to see real change in behaviour, if the goals are clear. Plan around Ramadan and the summer. And at the end, ask the leader, the manager and a few people around the leader whether they have noticed a difference. That turns coaching from a perk into an investment you can explain.
