What AI changes for leaders in the Gulf, and what it doesn't

A machine can draft the decision. It can't sign it, and it can't be held accountable when it's wrong.

By Hisham Elsaied, executive coach and author, Dubai  ·  November 2026

An HR director finishes her workforce plan in forty minutes with an AI tool. The same document used to take her team two weeks. In the executive meeting the CEO reads it and asks, quite sincerely: "If a machine can do this in an hour, why do I need an HR department?"

She is a composite of several leaders, but I hear some version of that question almost every week now.

The scale of the change

The World Economic Forum's Future of Jobs Report 2025 expects disruption to touch 22% of jobs by 2030, with nearly 40% of the skills people use at work changing. The Gulf is moving faster than most. By late 2025, Microsoft's research put the UAE first in the world for AI use, with around six in ten people of working age using AI tools.

What leaders actually bring to coaching

  • A quiet fear. Not about the technology, but about whether their team, or their own role, will look the same in two years.
  • Pressure to decide faster. If a tool can draft the strategy before lunch, why does the decision still take a week?
  • Confident answers, uncertain people. Leaders ask an AI tool for advice, get a clear answer, and then lie awake unsure whether to trust it.

None of these is a technology problem. They are human problems that technology has made louder.

What stays human

In my book The Moving Line I argue that every time a machine does something we thought only a mind could do, we move the line. Chess was thinking until a computer won. Then it was calculation. Each time, people feel the floor tilt.

But the more of a leader's work is shared with machines, the more the human part of the job matters. Reading what isn't said in the room. Understanding the politics, the culture and the people around a decision. Building the trust that lets someone say "I think we're wrong" to a senior colleague. And carrying the responsibility when the decision goes badly, because nobody can fire the machine.

Three questions for leaders and HR

  1. Which part of your work has already moved to machines, and what have you done with the time it freed?
  2. What can you do for your organisation that the plan can't?
  3. When you last used AI to help with a decision, did it leave you more sure, or less? What does that tell you?
