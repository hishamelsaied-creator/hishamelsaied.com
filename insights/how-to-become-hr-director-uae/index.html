How to become an HR director in the UAE and the Gulf

What actually gets people appointed, what holds good HR managers back, and a plan for the next twelve months.

By Hisham Elsaied, former regional HR director and executive coach, Dubai  ·  November 2026

In short: most HR directors in the Gulf get the job because a CEO or general manager trusts their judgement on the business, not because of one more certificate. Qualifications help you get shortlisted. What decides the appointment is whether senior people already see you as one of them, and whether one of them speaks for you when the role opens.

I spent twenty years in HR, ending as a regional HR director, and I coach alongside that work. Many of the people I coach now are HR managers and business partners who are good at their jobs and can't see why the next step isn't happening. The reasons are usually fixable, but they are rarely the ones people expect.

How the director role is different

A senior HR manager runs HR well. An HR director answers to the CEO, and often the board, for whether the business has the people it needs to deliver its plan. That changes three things.

  • You're judged on business results. Nobody on the leadership team asks how many training hours were delivered. They ask whether the organisation has the leaders it needs for next year's plan, and what it will cost.
  • You lead leaders. Your team includes managers who are experts in areas you are not. Your job is to set direction and make decisions, not to be the best specialist in the room.
  • You make trade-offs in public. Restructures, pay decisions, a senior leader who has to go. You will disappoint someone in most weeks, and you have to be able to explain why.

What the research says HR leaders are valued for

One of the longest-running studies of HR competencies is the HR Competency Study run by the RBL Group and the University of Michigan's Ross School, led by Dave Ulrich. Its 2007 round surveyed more than 10,000 HR professionals and line managers in over 400 companies across eight regions. The most valued quality was being a "credible activist": someone line leaders trust, who also takes a position on the business and not only on HR matters. The same round found that the skills of a company's HR people accounted for about 20 percent of its business results.

Later rounds have added new themes, such as leading change and using technology, but credibility with line leaders keeps coming back. That matches what I saw from the inside. The HR managers who were promoted were the ones the business leaders already asked for advice.

What separates the people who get the role

  1. They talk about the business first. They can read a profit and loss statement, they know the three numbers the CEO worries about, and they describe HR work in those terms. "This retention plan protects the project pipeline" lands better than "this improves engagement".
  2. They have led one big piece of work from start to finish. A restructure, a merger, a new performance system, a nationalisation plan. Something with real risk that they owned themselves.
  3. They've handled a hard situation well. A senior investigation, a redundancy programme, a conflict between two executives. Boards want to know you stay calm and fair when it gets difficult.
  4. They have a sponsor. A mentor gives advice. A sponsor is a senior person who puts your name forward and backs you in rooms you are not in.
  5. They understand the local context. In the Gulf that means labour law, nationalisation programmes such as Emiratisation, working with government and semi-government entities, and leading teams from many nationalities. Directors are expected to know where the lines are.

The common routes in the Gulf

There is no single path, but most HR directors I know came through one of three.

  • Business partner to head of HR to director. The most common. Years spent close to a business unit give you the commercial sense directors need.
  • Specialist to generalist. Reward, talent or learning specialists who deliberately take a generalist or business partner role before going for the top job. Staying a specialist too long is one of the most common blocks I see.
  • A regional role in a multinational. Covering several countries teaches you to work across cultures and legal systems, which Gulf employers value.

A senior professional qualification, such as CIPD Level 7 or SHRM-SCP, is often listed in director job adverts in the region. It helps you get through the first screen. It won't make up for a missing track record, and you don't need a master's degree. Many successful HR directors don't have one.

A twelve-month plan

  1. Months 1 to 2. Ask your manager, and one senior leader outside HR, what they would need to see from you to consider you ready for a director role. Write down their answers word for word.
  2. Months 2 to 4. Learn the business numbers. Sit with finance for an hour and go through the budget. Find out what drives revenue and cost in your organisation.
  3. Months 3 to 8. Volunteer to lead one piece of work that the CEO cares about, even if it is uncomfortable. Report on it in business terms.
  4. Months 6 to 10. Build your visibility. Present your work to the leadership team yourself. Speak at one external event or write one article on what you've learned.
  5. Months 9 to 12. Have a direct conversation with your sponsor about your next step, and ask what is still missing.

The mistakes that hold people back

  • Waiting to be noticed. Good work is necessary but rarely enough. If senior people don't know what you've done, it didn't happen as far as promotion is concerned.
  • Speaking HR language to business leaders. Terms like "competency framework" and "talent pipeline" mean little to a CFO. Describe the problem you solve and what it is worth.
  • Saying yes to everything. Directors decide what not to do. Showing that you can push back, with reasons, is part of being seen as ready.
  • Avoiding conflict with senior people. If you have never disagreed with a senior leader in a meeting, nobody knows how you'll hold the line when it matters.

Questions to ask yourself

  1. Which senior leader outside HR would describe me as someone they rely on, and why?
  2. What is the biggest piece of work I have owned, and what did it change for the business?
  3. Who would put my name forward if a director role opened tomorrow?

Common questions

What qualifications do you need to become an HR director in the UAE?

There is no single required qualification. Director adverts in the region often list a senior professional qualification such as CIPD Level 7 or SHRM-SCP, which helps you pass the first screen. What decides the appointment is a track record of leading significant work, credibility with business leaders, and a senior person who backs you. A master's degree is not required.

How long does it take to become an HR director?

It varies a great deal. Years of experience matter less than whether you have business fluency, have owned a major piece of work, and have handled difficult situations well. People who deliberately build those tend to move faster than those who wait.

What is the difference between an HR manager and an HR director?

An HR manager runs HR processes and a team well. An HR director answers to the CEO, and often the board, for whether the organisation has the people it needs to deliver its plan, leads other leaders and makes trade-offs that affect the whole business.

Does an HR director in the Gulf need to speak Arabic?

Not for most private sector roles, but Arabic is a real advantage in government and semi-government entities and in many family businesses. Understanding local labour law and nationalisation programmes such as Emiratisation matters in every sector.

Can a career coach help me become an HR director?

Yes, if you use coaching to work on what is actually holding you back, such as how senior leaders see you, a missing piece of experience, or how you present your work.
