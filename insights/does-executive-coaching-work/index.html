Does executive coaching work? What the research actually says

HR directors are right to ask for evidence before they spend on coaching. Here is what the strongest studies show, where the evidence is weaker than coaches like to admit, and what it means when you buy.

By Hisham Elsaied, ICF PCC executive coach in Dubai  ·  November 2026

When a finance director asks me whether coaching works, I don't start with client stories. Stories are easy to find for anything. The better question is what happens when researchers compare people who were coached with similar people who weren't. That research now exists in reasonable quantity, and it gives a clear, if not perfect, answer.

The short answer

  • g = 0.59average effect of workplace coaching across 37 randomised controlled trials, a moderate effect (de Haan and Nilsson, 2023)
  • 2,528people in those trials, published between 1994 and 2021
  • δ = 0.36overall effect on organisational outcomes in an earlier meta-analysis of 17 studies (Jones, Woods and Guillaume, 2016)
  • r = 0.41link between the quality of the coach and client relationship and the results, across 27 samples (Graßmann et al., 2020)

In plain terms: coaching, on average, helps. The effect is moderate, which in the world of workplace interventions is respectable. Many leadership programmes can't show as much.

Why randomised trials matter

Most coaching evaluations ask clients afterwards whether it helped. People who chose coaching, enjoyed it and spent their company's money on it tend to say yes. That tells you little.

A randomised controlled trial assigns people to coaching or to a comparison group by chance, so the two groups start out alike. Erik de Haan and Viktor Nilsson pulled together every such trial of workplace and executive coaching they could find in English. Thirty-seven met their standard. Across them, coaching had a statistically significant positive effect on leadership and personal outcomes.

Where the evidence is weaker

I'd rather you hear this from a coach than discover it later. The same authors found signs of publication bias: studies with positive results are more likely to be published than studies that found nothing. Correcting for that lowers the estimate. The true effect is probably somewhat smaller than 0.59, though still positive.

The outcomes also differ in strength. In the Jones, Woods and Guillaume meta-analysis, coaching had its largest effects on individual results and on attitudes such as confidence and motivation (δ = 0.51), and smaller effects on measured skills (δ = 0.28). So coaching changes how people feel and what they achieve more readily than it builds a technical skill. That's another reason coaching and training aren't interchangeable.

Two findings from that study surprised many coaches. Internal coaches showed stronger effects than external ones, and adding 360-degree feedback produced smaller effects, not larger. Neither finding means external coaching or 360s are a bad idea. They do mean that more inputs and a bigger name don't automatically produce more change.

The relationship is the strongest single factor

Across 27 samples covering more than 3,500 coaching relationships, the quality of the working alliance between coach and client was clearly linked to results (r = 0.41). It was also linked to fewer unwanted side effects (r = −0.29). The type of client and the coach's experience didn't change that link.

This is why I always suggest that leaders meet more than one coach before deciding, and why a chemistry conversation is not a formality. If the client doesn't trust the coach, the method won't rescue it.

What this means when you buy coaching

For an HR director in the Gulf, I'd take five practical points from the research.

  • Expect a real but moderate effect. Coaching is not a cure for a poor appointment or a broken team. It helps capable people get better faster.
  • Write down the outcome before you start. Agree in a three-way meeting with the leader and their manager what should be different in six months, and how you'll know.
  • Let the leader choose from two or three coaches. The relationship drives results, and choice builds commitment.
  • Check readiness. A leader who has been sent, and doesn't see an issue, is a poor bet. Have the honest conversation first.
  • Measure something beyond satisfaction. Ask the manager and two or three colleagues at the start and the end. Their view is worth more than the coachee's rating of the coach.

A note on AI coaching

AI coaching tools are improving quickly, and early studies are mixed. I've written separately about what the controlled studies found so far. For now, the strong evidence base belongs to human coaching.

Sources

  • Jones, R. J., Woods, S. A., and Guillaume, Y. R. F. (2016). The effectiveness of workplace coaching: a meta-analysis of learning and performance outcomes from coaching. Journal of Occupational and Organizational Psychology, 89(2), 249–277.
