Psychological safety in Gulf teams: what leaders can actually do

Teams do better when people can raise problems and admit mistakes without fear. In workplaces where seniority carries real weight, that doesn't happen by itself. It has to be built, mostly by the leader.

By Hisham Elsaied, ICF PCC executive coach in Dubai  ·  November 2026

Psychological safety has become one of those phrases that appears in every engagement survey and every leadership competency framework. That's a pity, because the idea behind it is precise and useful, and the research on it is strong. Used well, it's one of the most practical things a leader can work on.

What it means

Amy Edmondson at Harvard defines psychological safety as a shared belief in a team that it's safe to take interpersonal risks: to ask a question that might sound naive, to admit a mistake, to disagree with someone more senior, or to raise a problem early. It isn't about being nice, and it isn't the absence of pressure or high standards. A team can have demanding goals and still be a place where people say what they see.

What the research says

  • 136independent samples, more than 22,000 people and nearly 5,000 groups, in a large meta-analysis of psychological safety (Frazier et al., 2017)
  • 180teams studied by Google's Project Aristotle, which found psychological safety the most important of five factors in team effectiveness

The 2017 meta-analysis by Lance Frazier and colleagues confirmed that psychological safety is linked to learning, performance and engagement, and that leadership behaviour is one of its main drivers. Google's internal study of its own teams reached a similar conclusion: who was on the team mattered less than how the team worked together, and psychological safety came out as the foundation for the other four factors.

Why it's harder here, and why it matters more

Most Gulf organisations I work with have a clear sense of hierarchy, and respect for seniority is part of the culture, not a flaw in it. The side effect is that bad news travels upward slowly. People wait to be asked. They agree in the meeting and raise concerns afterwards, privately, if at all. Many teams are also highly international, so people bring different assumptions about whether it's acceptable to challenge a manager.

The cost shows up in predictable places: problems that surface late, projects that everyone privately doubted, and new leaders who don't hear the truth for months. In fast-growing organisations, those delays are expensive.

The answer isn't to copy a flat Silicon Valley culture. It's for leaders to make speaking up a normal, expected part of how their team works, in a way that fits the respect people already show.

What leaders can do this month

  • Ask before you tell. In meetings, invite views before you give yours. Once the most senior person has spoken, most people stop thinking about alternatives.
  • Ask specific questions. "Any comments?" invites silence. "What would make this plan fail?" or "What are we not seeing?" gives people permission to raise doubts.
  • Thank the messenger, every time. The first time someone brings you bad news, your reaction is watched by the whole team. Thank them before you deal with the problem.
  • Admit your own mistakes out loud. When a senior leader says "I got that wrong", it lowers the cost of honesty for everyone below them.
  • Create a private route. Some people will never challenge you in a group, and that's fine. Regular one-to-ones with the question "What should I know that I'm not hearing?" give them another way.
  • Separate the person from the problem. When something goes wrong, ask what happened and what we'll change, before asking who. Teams learn fast which question comes first.

A note for HR

If your engagement survey includes an item on speaking up, look at the scores by team rather than for the whole organisation. Psychological safety is a team-level property, and two teams in the same department can be completely different. The gap usually comes down to the manager. That makes it a coaching and development issue, not a policy one.
