Executive coaching in the Gulf: what the numbers say

Coaching is growing faster in the Middle East than almost anywhere else. Here are the figures that matter if you're about to hire a coach, and what I'd take from them.

By Hisham Elsaied, ICF PCC executive coach in Dubai  ·  November 2026

HR directors often ask me two questions before they hire a coach: is everyone doing this now, and what should it cost? The best data we have comes from the International Coaching Federation's Global Coaching Study, which PwC runs for the ICF every few years. The 2025 edition drew on more than 10,000 responses from 127 countries.

The numbers in short

  • 122,974coach practitioners worldwide in 2025, a record (ICF 2025)
  • US$5.34 billionglobal coaching revenue, up 17% on 2023 (ICF 2025)
  • US$234average fee for a one-hour session worldwide, about AED 860 (ICF 2025)
  • 72%the increase in client numbers reported for the Middle East and Africa, the highest of any region (ICF 2025)
  • 71%of coaches in the Middle East and Africa expect their coaching income to grow over the next year, also the highest (ICF 2025)
  • +74%growth in the number of coaches in the Middle East and Africa between 2019 and 2022, to about 4,900 (ICF 2023)

1. More coaches means a wider range of quality

The number of coaches in our region grew by roughly three quarters in three years. Some of that growth is experienced leaders and HR people who trained properly and coach well. Some of it is people who took a short online course and added "coach" to their LinkedIn headline. Both show up in the same search results.

So the credential matters more here than in a mature market. ICF has three levels, and each one needs a set number of hours coaching real clients: 100 hours for ACC, 500 for PCC and 2,500 for MCC, with recorded sessions assessed along the way. Ask which level a coach holds and check it on the ICF directory. It takes two minutes.

2. The average fee tells you little about executive coaching

The global average of about US$234 an hour covers every kind of coaching: life, wellness, career and executive. Executive coaching for directors and senior leaders usually costs more, because the coach needs business experience as well as coaching skill, and because organisations usually buy it as a programme of several sessions rather than a single hour.

A more useful question than "what does an hour cost?" is "what will be different in three months, and how will we know?" If a coach can't answer that, the hourly rate doesn't matter.

3. Demand is rising fastest here

The Middle East and Africa reported the biggest increase in client numbers of any region, and its coaches were the most optimistic about the year ahead. That matches what I see in the Gulf. Organisations are growing quickly, nationalisation targets are moving people into senior roles earlier, and more leaders than ever are stepping into jobs that are bigger than the ones they trained for.

For HR, that means good coaches get booked up. If you know a leader will need support in a new role, start looking before the appointment is announced, not after the first difficult quarter.

4. Language and culture aren't in the global numbers

The global study doesn't tell you whether a coach can work in Arabic, or understands how decisions are made in a government entity or a family business. In the Gulf those two things often decide whether coaching works. I've written separately about why the language of the session matters and about six checks before you hire a coach in the UAE.
