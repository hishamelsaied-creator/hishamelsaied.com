Coaching in Arabic: why the language of the session matters

Many leaders in the Gulf work in English and feel in Arabic. A coaching session can use both.

By Hisham Elsaied, Arabic-speaking executive coach in Dubai  ·  November 2026

A manager once spent forty minutes describing a problem with her boss in calm, professional English. "There's a misalignment in expectations," she said. It was a very tidy account. Then I asked her, in Arabic, how it felt when he corrected her in front of her team. Her voice changed, and in two minutes we were talking about the real problem.

The people in that story are a composite, but I see that moment often. Most of the leaders I coach live in two languages. They write emails and present to the board in English. They argue with their families, comfort their children and think through the hardest decisions in Arabic.

What the research says

In 2012 a team at the University of Chicago led by the psychologist Boaz Keysar published a study called "The Foreign Language Effect". They found that people making decisions in a second language were less affected by some common thinking biases. Their explanation was that a second language creates more distance, and carries less emotional weight than the language you grew up with.

That fits what I see in coaching. English is often the language of the role: structured and a little careful. Arabic, for native speakers, is often closer to the person. Values, family and feelings come through more easily.

How I use both

  • Arabic to find what matters. When an account sounds like a report, I may ask the next question in Arabic and see where the client goes.
  • English to decide what to do. When someone is caught up in a strong feeling, moving into English for a few minutes can give them the distance to look at their options.
  • Words that don't translate. Words like واجب (duty) or عيب (what's improper, with a social weight that "shame" misses) carry more than their English versions. When a client reaches for one, it's usually worth stopping to ask what it means to them.

Do you need an Arabic-speaking coach?

Not always. Many excellent coaches here don't speak Arabic, and their clients get a lot from them. But if the work touches confidence, family expectations, reputation or a difficult relationship, being able to go into Arabic can turn a good session into a real breakthrough. If you're in HR choosing a coach for a leader, it is worth asking which language the leader thinks in when the stakes are high.
