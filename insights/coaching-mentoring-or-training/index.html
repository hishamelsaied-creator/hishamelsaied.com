Coaching, mentoring or training: which one do you actually need?

They're often bought for the wrong reason. Here's a simple way to tell them apart.

By Hisham Elsaied, executive coach and author, Dubai  ·  November 2026

Here's a situation I see often. An HR manager asks for a coach for a new head of finance. When you talk it through, what he needs was someone who had run a finance function before to show him the ropes, and a short course on presenting to the board. Coaching would help later. It isn't the first thing he needs.

This happens a lot. The three words get used as if they mean the same thing, and money is spent on the wrong one.

The short version

  • Training teaches a skill or a body of knowledge. The trainer knows the content and the learner doesn't yet. It works best when the gap is clear and the same for many people.
  • Mentoring passes on experience. A more senior person shares what they've learned, opens doors and gives advice. It works best when someone is new to a world the mentor knows well.
  • Coaching helps a person think, decide and act differently in their own situation. The International Coaching Federation defines it as partnering with clients in a thought-provoking and creative process that inspires them to maximise their personal and professional potential. The coach doesn't need to have done the client's job, and doesn't give the answers.

Which one fits

Ask what is really in the way.

  • "I don't know how to do it." That's usually training. Someone who has never built a workforce plan needs to learn how.
  • "I don't know how things work here, or at this level." That's often mentoring. A first-time director benefits from someone who has been one in the same kind of organisation.
  • "I know what to do, and I'm still not doing it," or "I'm not sure what I want." That's coaching. The issue is judgement, confidence, relationships or a decision, and only the person can work it out.

Most senior leaders need some of all three over a career. The mistake is using one to solve a problem that belongs to another. Training won't fix a strained relationship with a CEO. Coaching won't teach someone employment law.

Where the lines blur

Many good coaches have real experience of their clients' world, and it helps them ask better questions. In my own work, having been a regional HR director means I understand the pressure HR leaders are under. But a coaching session where I spend the hour telling someone what I would do isn't coaching. It's mentoring with a different label. A good coach will tell you when you need something else.

Questions to ask before you buy

  1. What will this person be doing differently in six months, and how will we know?
  2. Is the gap knowledge, experience or judgement?
  3. Who is best placed to close it: a trainer, a senior colleague or a coach?
